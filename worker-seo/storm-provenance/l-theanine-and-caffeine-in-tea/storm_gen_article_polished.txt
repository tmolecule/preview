# Chemical Composition

## L-Theanine

L-Theanine, also known as **L-γ-glutamylethylamide**, **N5-ethyl-L-glutamine**, or **γ-glutamylethylamide**, is a bioactive, non-proteinogenic amino acid[1]. Its molecular formula is C₇H₁₄N₂O₃, with a molecular weight of 174.20[2][3]. It appears as a white to off-white solid[2] and is structurally similar to the proteinogenic amino acids glutamic acid and L-glutamine[1].
Chemically, L-theanine is a derivative of glutamine that is ethylated on the amide nitrogen, or alternatively described as the amide formed from ethylamine and L-glutamic acid at its γ-(5-) side chain carboxylic acid group[1]. As a structural analog of glutamate and glutamine, L-theanine is absorbed in the small intestine after oral ingestion, with hydrolysis to L-glutamate and ethylamine occurring in both the intestine and liver[1]. L-theanine constitutes approximately 1–2% of the dry weight of green tea leaves[1].

## Caffeine

Caffeine is a well-known stimulant compound with a molecular formula of C₈H₁₀N₄O₂ and a molecular weight of 194.19[4][5]. In its pure form, caffeine appears as an odorless white powder[4]. Caffeine was first isolated from black coffee by Pierre Jean Robiquet in 1821[6], and its presence in tea was discovered in 1827, at which time it was initially referred to as "theine" before being recognized as the same compound[7][8].
Caffeine primarily acts in the central nervous system by blocking adenosine receptors, which in turn enhances the secretion of neurotransmitters such as dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin, thereby improving alertness, mood, and motivation[9]. Additionally, caffeine influences cellular calcium transport and inhibits enzymes responsible for breaking down cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), further contributing to its performance-enhancing effects[9].

# Occurrence in Tea

Both L-theanine and caffeine occur naturally in the leaves of *Camellia sinensis*, the plant from which white, green, oolong, black, and pu-erh teas are derived.[10] Caffeine was first isolated from tea in 1827 by a chemist named Oudry, though it was initially believed to be a distinct compound from the caffeine found in coffee before later research confirmed they were identical.[8][11] L-theanine was discovered considerably later, first identified as a component of green tea in 1949, and was subsequently extracted for the first time from gyokuro leaves in 1950.[12]
L-theanine is chemically known as γ-glutamylethylamide and is classified as an amino acid analogue of L-glutamate and L-glutamine, possessing an ethyl group at the amide nitrogen.[13][14] Its molecular formula is C₇H₁₄N₂O₃.[15] Caffeine, by contrast, has a molecular formula of C₈H₁₀N₄O₂ and occurs in its pure form as an odorless white powder.[4]
The concentrations of both compounds vary considerably across different tea types. Studies analyzing commercial white, green, oolong, black, and pu-erh teas have found that while caffeine content is broadly similar across categories, theanine content differs more significantly.[16] Unfermented teas tend to contain higher concentrations of L-theanine, while more heavily fermented teas such as pu-erh contain practically none.[16] When steaming is avoided during the processing of green tea, the resulting product can be particularly rich in theanine, exceeding 2% theanine content in some cases.[16] Shaded green teas such as matcha and gyokuro are especially notable for their high L-theanine content, as the shading process is specifically employed to enhance its accumulation in the leaves.[17]
The natural co-occurrence of caffeine and L-theanine in tea is considered significant, as the two compounds are believed to work in a complementary fashion. The most-studied ratio of the two compounds, approximating that found naturally in shaded green teas, is 1 part caffeine to 2 parts L-theanine.[17] Despite this general pattern, considerable variability exists even within tea categories, meaning tea type alone cannot reliably predict the concentration of either compound in a given sample.[16]

# Mechanisms of Action

## L-Theanine

L-theanine, chemically known as γ-glutamylethylamide, is an amino acid analogue primarily found in green tea leaves (*Camellia sinensis*) and to a lesser extent in certain species of mushrooms[14]. After oral intake, L-theanine crosses the blood-brain barrier via the leucine-preferring transport system, which regulates neutral amino acid transport[18][19]. Once in the brain, L-theanine exerts its calming and anxiolytic effects through several mechanisms. It promotes relaxation and reduces stress by modulating neurotransmitter activity, including enhancing the activity of GABA, which is known for its calming effects and ability to reduce feelings of anxiety[14]. Additionally, L-theanine competes for glutamate receptors, including NMDA, AMPA, and KA receptors, which contributes further to its anti-stress action[20]. L-theanine also increases alpha brain wave activity in the brain, which is associated with relaxation and selective attention[18].

## Caffeine

Caffeine is a central nervous system (CNS) stimulant of the methylxanthine class and is the most commonly consumed psychoactive substance globally[21]. Its primary mode of action involves blocking the binding of adenosine to its receptors, which are mainly distributed across different parts of the brain[22][21]. By preventing adenosine from binding to its receptors, caffeine enhances the secretion of neurotransmitters such as dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin, thereby improving alertness, mood, and motivation[9]. Additionally, caffeine plays a role in cellular calcium transport, which regulates neurotransmitter secretion in neurons and contractile force in muscle cells, and it also inhibits enzymes that break down cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), further contributing to its performance-enhancing effects[9].

## Combined Mechanisms

When L-theanine and caffeine are consumed together, as is naturally the case in tea, their distinct mechanisms of action interact in complementary ways[23][24]. L-theanine has been found to counteract some of caffeine's less desirable effects, such as tending to reduce caffeine-induced rises in blood pressure, without diminishing caffeine's beneficial increases in alertness[25]. The calming influence of L-theanine, mediated through its effects on GABA, glutamate receptors, and alpha brain wave activity, helps to offset the jitteriness sometimes associated with caffeine[18][24]. Rather than blunting caffeine's cognitive benefits, L-theanine appears to enhance certain aspects of caffeine's effects on cognitive function, particularly attention[26]. This synergy results in what is often described as a state of "calm focus" or "relaxed alertness," making the combination more effective for cognitive performance than either substance alone[27].

# L-Theanine and Caffeine in Tea

L-theanine and caffeine are two pharmacologically active compounds that occur naturally and simultaneously in the leaves of *Camellia sinensis*, the plant from which white, green, oolong, black, and pu-erh teas are derived.[10] L-theanine (γ-glutamylethylamide) is a non-proteinogenic amino acid with the molecular formula C₇H₁₄N₂O₃, first identified as a component of green tea in 1949 and subsequently isolated from gyokuro leaves in 1950.[12] Caffeine, a methylxanthine stimulant with the molecular formula C₈H₁₀N₄O₂, was isolated from tea in 1827, at which time it was initially referred to as "theine" before being recognized as chemically identical to the caffeine found in coffee.[7][8]
The two compounds differ substantially in their individual mechanisms of action. Caffeine primarily functions as a central nervous system stimulant by blocking adenosine receptors, thereby promoting the release of neurotransmitters such as dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin, which enhance alertness, mood, and motivation.[9] L-theanine, by contrast, exerts calming and anxiolytic effects by enhancing GABAergic activity, competing at glutamate receptors, and increasing alpha brain wave activity associated with relaxed attention.[18][20]
When consumed together, as naturally occurs in tea, the two compounds are widely regarded as interacting synergistically. L-theanine has been found to attenuate some of caffeine's less desirable effects — such as elevated blood pressure and jitteriness — without diminishing its cognitive benefits, while also enhancing certain aspects of caffeine's effects on attention.[25][26] This interaction is commonly described as producing a state of "calm focus" or "relaxed alertness," and the most-studied ratio approximating natural concentrations in shaded green teas is 1 part caffeine to 2 parts L-theanine.[17][27]
The concentrations of both compounds vary considerably across and within tea types. Shaded green teas such as matcha and gyokuro are particularly high in L-theanine, as the shading process promotes its accumulation in the leaves, while heavily fermented teas such as pu-erh contain practically none.[16][17] Caffeine content, by contrast, is broadly similar across tea categories.[16] Because of this variability, tea type alone cannot reliably predict the precise concentration of either compound in a given sample.[16]
